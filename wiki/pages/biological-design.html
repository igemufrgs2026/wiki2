Overview

❯❯❯

Aiming to uncover the hidden pathways and local sources through which 2,4-D drifts and reaches nearby farms, our team engineered an existing biological pathway that specifically recognizes this herbicide in bacteria and adapted it for detection outside living cells. By coupling this recognition system to a detectable signal, we aim to make this otherwise invisible threat visible, providing farmers with a practical tool to identify 2,4-D contamination in the field.

The design

What was our cassette based on?

With that objective in mind, we searched the literature for organisms that naturally interact with 2,4-D and, particularly, for biological systems capable of sensing the compound. This search led us to Bradyrhizobium sp. strain HW13, a bacteria previously described as a 2,4-D degrader [1]. In this organism, the cad gene cluster contains the regulatory elements associated with 2,4-D degradation, including the positive transcriptional regulator CadR and the cadA promoter (pCadA). We were particularly interested in these components because they provided a way of separating the natural recognition of 2,4-D from the degradation pathway itself. In summary there were basically 8 key points in that article [2]:

  1. Developed a whole-cell biosensor based on the cadR/cadA system from Bradyrhizobium sp. HW13.
  2. Transferred the regulatory system to Agrobacterium tumefaciens NTL4.
  3. Coupled the 2,4-D-responsive pCadA promoter to a reporter system based on formylglycine-generating enzyme and arylsulfatase.
  4. Mechanism: presence of 2,4-D activates cadR → transcription of the cassette → expression of a sulfatase enzyme → cleavage of 4-MUS → luminescence under UV light.
  5. Improved the system through directed evolution of CadR (error-prone PCR), obtaining variants with increased sensitivity to 2,4-D.
  6. Best-performing mutant detected concentrations as low as 1.56 μM, an eight-fold improvement over the wild-type system.
  7. The system could distinguish 2,4-D from several unrelated environmental contaminants, supporting its specificity.
  8. Relevance to the project: showed that the natural regulatory system from Bradyrhizobium could be transferred to another biological context while retaining its ability to respond to 2,4-D.

Instead of reproducing their entire metabolic pathway required to break down the herbicide, we could use the regulatory mechanism that naturally responds to its presence and connect it to a different output. This approach allowed us to transform a biological mechanism originally involved in pollutant degradation into a sensing system. The discovery of this regulatory pathway therefore became the foundation for our biosensor design, with CadR as our main protein of interest and the cadA promoter as the regulatory element responsible for translating the recognition of 2,4-D into gene expression.

Based on these findings, we decided to use the cadR/pcadA system as the biological foundation of our own biosensor. However, rather than reproducing the whole-cell system described in the previous study, our goal was to adapt this mechanism to a cell-free platform. This change allows us to keep the key biological feature that we were looking for, which is the specific recognition of 2,4-D by CadR and the consequent activation of the cadA promoter, while removing the need to maintain living cells in the final detection system. For us, this was particularly important because it ensures safety and practicality for the farmers to operate the hardware. By combining the natural sensing mechanism identified in Bradyrhizobium with a cell-free expression system and a detectable reporter, we aim to convert the presence of 2,4-D into a clear signal that can be measured without requiring specialized laboratory infrastructure.

Our own pathway explained

The CadR protein has been characterized as a positive transcriptional regulator of the cadA promoter in response to 2,4-D. However, the molecular mechanism underlying this regulation has not yet been fully elucidated. Based on the behavior of characterized members of the AraC/XylS family, to which CadR belongs, it can be hypothesized that 2,4-D acts as a signal molecule that interacts with a regulatory domain of CadR, inducing a conformational change that promotes its interaction with the cadA regulatory region and facilitates transcriptional activation [3]. Nevertheless, this mechanism remains theoretical for CadR and requires experimental validation, as the molecular responses to environmental signals can differ substantially among members of the AraC/XylS family [4]. Based on this natural regulatory mechanism, we designed our own pathway to convert the transcriptional response to 2,4-D into a directly detectable signal. As it follows, we designed the piGEM_CadA/CadR-apt with the pcadA and CadR regulating the transcription of an aptamer as a reporter, rather than relying on traditional reporter protein commonly applied in biosensors [5], thus fastening the reaction time and simplifying the cell free reaction. Also, another element called RiboJ was added in the cassette, it acts as an insulator to amplify the number of total mRNA and to prevent the translation of the cassette.

CadR is the regulatory protein of the system, constitutively expressed under the strong J23119 promoter, with an RBS enabling its translation and a synthetic terminator ending its cassette. Its function is to act as the sensor: in the absence of 2,4-D, CadR represses transcription from the pCadA promoter; upon binding 2,4-D, it undergoes a conformational change that relieves this repression, activating transcription downstream.

pCadA is the 2,4-D-responsive promoter that drives the detection cassette. Its activity is directly controlled by CadR — repressed when CadR is unbound, and activated when CadR binds 2,4-D. It functions as the input switch of the system, converting the presence of the herbicide into a transcriptional signal.

RiboJ is a self-cleaving ribozyme insulator placed immediately downstream of pCadA. Its function is to standardize the 5′ end of the transcript, increasing downstream RNA abundance and producing a stronger, more detectable signal, as previously described [6]. It also insulates the reporter RNA from upstream promoter context and prevents its eventual translation, which is important since this cassette is designed to produce RNA only, not protein.

3WJdB is the reporter element of the system. Once transcribed, this RNA aptamer folds into a structure that specifically interacts with the fluorescent dye DHFB-I. Its function is to convert the transcriptional output of the pCadA cassette into a measurable fluorescent signal, without requiring translation into a protein. The DHFB-I is the fluorescent dye used for detection [7]. Its function is to bind specifically to the 3WJdB aptamer once it is transcribed, generating a fluorescence signal proportional to the amount of reporter RNA produced — and therefore proportional to pathway activation by 2,4-D.

A strong constitutive promoter [8] that continuously drives transcription of the CadR coding sequence, independent of 2,4-D.

Ribosome binding site enabling translation of the CadR coding sequence into functional protein.

Stops transcription at the end of the CadR sequence, preventing read-through into downstream elements.

❯❯❯

Our biosensor plasmid consists of two cassettes: a constitutive CadR expression cassette, composed of the J23119 promoter, a ribosome binding site, the CadR coding sequence, and a synthetic terminator, which continuously produces the CadR regulatory protein responsible for sensing 2,4-D, and a detection cassette, composed of the pCadA promoter, the RiboJ insulator, the 3WJdB aptamer, and the same synthetic terminator, in which pCadA is repressed by CadR in the absence of 2,4-D and activated upon CadR-2,4-D binding, RiboJ standardizes the transcript's 5′ end to boost RNA abundance and prevent unwanted translation, and the 3WJdB aptamer serves as an RNA reporter that specifically binds the fluorescent dye DHFB-I to generate a signal proportional to pathway activation, measured with our portable field spectrophotometer; by relying on an RNA reporter rather than a protein, transcription itself becomes the final output, allowing the reporter RNA to be produced in approximately 15 minutes and enabling a rapid, cell-free platform for detecting 2,4-D contamination in the field.

The system — CFS

Where the system is going to work (CFS)

The choice of a cell-free system was driven primarily by two considerations that are central to the development of our biosensor: biosafety and response time. Although whole-cell biosensors are a well-established approach for detecting environmental contaminants, the use of living genetically modified cells in a device intended for field application introduces additional biosafety and practical considerations. By removing the living organism from the final detection system, cell-free expression allows us to use the molecular machinery required for transcription and translation without releasing viable genetically modified microorganisms into the environment. This makes the system particularly attractive for a biosensor designed to be used directly in agricultural settings, where simplicity, containment, and ease of application are important.

In addition to this biosafety advantage, cell-free systems provide a rapid and highly controllable environment for gene expression because the genetic circuit operates outside the constraints of cellular growth, viability, and resource allocation. This characteristic is particularly relevant to our project because our goal is to obtain a rapid signal following exposure to 2,4-D rather than maintain long-term expression in a living cell. Cell-free expression systems have also been increasingly explored for molecular diagnostics and field-deployable biosensing because they can support the rapid prototyping and operation of genetic circuits without requiring the maintenance of living cells.

Our cell-free platform is based on an Escherichia coli extract, specifically derived from the Rosetta 2 strain. This choice follows the established use of engineered E. coli strains for cell-free protein synthesis, which has become one of the most widely used platforms due to the extensive characterization of its genetics and metabolism. The Rosetta 2 strain is derived from E. coli BL21 and contains additional plasmid-encoded rare tRNAs, which were introduced to aggregate more tRNAs in the reaction mixture to ensure correct translation of the CadR protein. The resulting extract provides the biological machinery necessary for gene expression, including the endogenous transcriptional and translational components of E. coli. However, the extract alone is not sufficient to sustain the reaction, so it is combined with a defined mixture of substrates and cofactors that recreate the conditions required for transcription and translation in vitro.

For our biosensor, this platform provides an additional advantage because the genetic circuit can be directly introduced into the reaction and immediately access the transcriptional machinery present in the extract. In our case, the plasmid containing the constitutive cadR expression cassette and the 2,4-D-responsive cadA reporter cassette is added as the DNA template, allowing the circuit to operate without the need to first transform or grow a living host. This is particularly important for our design because the final output is an RNA reporter rather than a reporter protein. By eliminating the need to produce a fluorescent protein, we can take advantage of the rapid transcriptional response of the cell-free system and directly couple the activation of the cadA promoter to the production of the RNA aptamer used for detection.

Final product

IN SUMMARY

In summary our team set out to make the invisible thread that the 2,4-D poses visible. The herbicide drifts quietly from farm to farm, often undetected until the damage is already done, and we wanted to give farmers a simple way to catch it before it spreads. So we turned to nature itself for the answer: buried in the genome of Bradyrhizobium sp. HW13, a soil bacterium that naturally breaks down 2,4-D, we found a regulatory system, cadR and its promoter pCadA, that already knew how to sense this herbicide. Rather than rebuilding the bacterium's entire degradation pathway, we borrowed just the part that recognizes 2,4-D and repurposed it as the foundation of our biosensor.

From there, we redesigned the pathway to fit our vision of a fast, safe, field-ready tool. We built a plasmid with two cassettes: one that constantly produces CadR, the sensor protein, and another where pCadA, once activated by 2,4-D, drives production of a glowing RNA aptamer instead of a traditional protein reporter. By skipping translation entirely and letting the RNA itself carry the signal, we cut the response time down to about 15 minutes. And by moving this whole system out of living cells and into a cell-free extract, we removed the risks and complications of deploying genetically modified organisms in the field. The result is a biosensor that takes a mechanism nature evolved for degradation and turns it into something new: a fast, safe, and practical way to catch 2,4-D contamination directly in the field.
