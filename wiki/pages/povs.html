For sensitive crop growers, drift threatens years of investment and family livelihoods. Early detection offers a way to identify contamination before visible symptoms appear.

Grape and Sensitive Crop Growers

For farmers cultivating grapevines, olives, and other sensitive crops, herbicide drift is an existential threat. Many have witnessed entire, multi-generational vineyards wither overnight, leaving them trapped in a cycle of grief and helplessness. Some farmers exhaust their resources trying to nurture poisoned vines, only to realize the damage is irreversible and be forced to cut them down completely. A courageous few attempt to replant, willingly absorbing the immense financial risk of losing everything again, while others abandon sensitive crops altogether. When introduced to our biosensor, their response was immediate enthusiasm. Many noted that during the critical application window (November to December), they would perform daily testing to detect drift early — before visual symptoms appear — viewing real-time monitoring as an indispensable tool to regain control over their livelihoods.

Consultations informing this perspectiveInterviews with 3 producers, and interactions with 50+ families at a workshop and a public hearing.

Soybean Producers

Grain growers operate in a high-stakes agricultural economy where they rely heavily on 2,4-D as a vital, cost-effective tool to control weeds. From their perspective, managing vast acreages without this herbicide is currently economically unviable. Many apply 2,4-D without intending to harm neighboring farms, often unaware of the extreme volatility of the compound or effect it may have on the nearby crops. Furthermore, strict compliance with label restrictions — such as specific wind speeds, humidity, and temperature thresholds — is difficult in Rio Grande do Sul, where optimal weather windows during application season are exceptionally rare. As a major economic driver in the region, soybean producers hold significant influence in local agricultural policy, advocating against outright bans on 2,4-D and favoring continued availability.

Consultations informing this perspectiveConsultations with state specialists and review of media content. This perspective was synthesized from these sources, rather than direct interviews with soybean producers.

Academic Researchers and Experts

While herbicide drift receives limited attention in broader academic circles, specialized researchers recognize it as a multifaceted socio-environmental challenge. Scholars emphasize that weather conditions in southern Brazil rarely align with ideal application parameters, making drift an almost inevitable byproduct of current practices. Researchers aim to quantify how 2,4-D impacts not only cultivated crops but also surrounding native flora and ecosystems, but express frustration over limited research funding and widespread public misinformation. They advocate for balancing the soybean sector's operational reliance on chemical tools with the survival of sensitive perennial agriculture. For them, continuous, low-cost field monitoring is the essential first step toward generating transparent data and developing sustainable co-existence strategies.

Consultations informing this perspectiveInterviews with 5 professors.

Agricultural Inspectors and Field Technicians

Positioned on the frontlines, technical inspectors serve as the primary bridge between devastated growers and alleged applicators. They observe the human toll of the crisis daily, yet operate within severe institutional constraints. Technicians are acutely aware that current legal mechanisms never compensate affected farmers, as establishing a strict, legally binding causal link back to a specific neighbor's sprayer is virtually impossible. Even when fines are issued, the revenue goes to state coffers rather than restoring the farmer’s losses. While maintaining strict professional neutrality and respecting institutional boundaries, many inspectors become deeply moved by the growers' plight. They recognize the systemic gaps in monitoring and view rapid, inexpensive diagnostic tools as a crucial asset to make field oversight meaningful.

Consultations informing this perspectiveInterviews with 10 technicians and relevant specialists.

Urban Communities and the General Public

The urban population remains largely disconnected from rural realities, possessing little awareness of the ongoing herbicide drift crisis. While many urban consumers express support for organic produce and harbor general concerns about pesticides, they often lack insight into the technical and economic dependencies of modern large-scale agriculture. When informed about the issue, citizens express genuine empathy for affected family farmers and hope for constructive solutions, yet feel empowered with little actionable ways to contribute. Their perspective highlights a broader societal divide, underscoring the urgent need for transparent science communication that connects agricultural innovation with public awareness.

Consultations informing this perspective246 responses to a public survey.
